> **TL;DR**: AI makes output cheap, and bad output too: slides no human would write, citations that are obviously wrong, documents clearly not meant for people to read. The cost of cleaning it up lands on whoever receives it. Managers don't need to ban AI or require labels. They need one bar that doesn't move: AI or not, the standard is the same, and the person who hands it in owns it.

## What AI garbage looks like

More and more of the documents I receive were obviously generated by AI and passed straight along.

The classic case is AI-made slides, full of things no human would actually write. Some models, afraid of being wrong, keep repeating caveats nobody needed.

Another is citations. Numbers and sources neatly listed, and when you click through, they don't match or don't exist.

And then there are documents that clearly weren't written for a person to read. Complete structure, nice formatting, but nobody reads a document that way or explains things that way.

They all share one trait: the person who made it spent very little time, and the person who receives it has to spend a lot of time to find the problems.

## The cost lands on the receiver

In March, Zety surveyed 1,000 US workers ([reported by Allwork.Space](https://allwork.space/2026/03/55-of-employees-receive-ai-workslop-from-managers-or-supervisors/)). 55% had received low-quality AI work from a manager or supervisor. 85% said it hurt their trust in the sender. About 45% said it made them more cautious about using AI at work.

Managers should read that twice. AI workslop isn't just a junior-staff problem. Managers send it too. And the cost isn't only time: the receiver starts to distrust the sender, and AI along with them.

The time the sender saved didn't disappear. It moved to the person who has to read it.

## When you receive it

I handle it one of two ways.

If I can ask the person to redo it, I tell them how it should be written and ask for another version in the structure I want. That's faster than fixing it myself, and they know the bar next time.

If I can't, for example when it comes from outside the company, I fight AI with AI: I use AI to help me read the document they made with AI, and to flag the key points and anything suspicious.

The second way solves the immediate problem, but it's a warning sign. When more and more documents in a company need AI just to get through, output is outpacing people's ability to judge it.

## Bans and labels aren't the answer

A manager's first instinct is often one of two things: ban AI, or require people to label what AI generated.

I don't think either is necessary.

A ban doesn't stop usage. It hides it.

Labels are subtler. Once people label AI work, they start adjusting the standard by it: "AI wrote this, so it's fine if it's rough," or the reverse, "AI wrote this, so I'll be extra strict." Either way, the quality bar moves with the tool.

When you judge quality, whether AI made it shouldn't be a reason for the bar to move.

## One standard: the person who hands it in owns it

What I do now is simple. I make the standard explicit:

Using AI is fine, but you check it. You can't hand me something poor and tell me it's poor because AI wrote it.

AI-made or self-made, I hold it to exactly the same bar. If it's bad, the person who handed it in is responsible.

The point of this rule isn't AI. It's ownership. AI makes output so easy that people forget handing something in is itself a promise: I've looked at this, and I think it's usable.

## What can ship directly, and what needs a person

"The person who hands it in owns it" doesn't mean a human reads every line. Here's how we split it at Codeer.

**Code is tiered by risk.** We grade code into risk levels. The higher the risk, the more it needs a person to review it. Low-risk code that passes acceptance checks and looks fine can ship.

**Anything written for people gets read by a person.** No debate here. There's no such thing as a document that goes straight to humans without anyone having read it.

Both rules come from the same judgment: the cost of being wrong decides how much human judgment you need. Let acceptance checks catch low-risk work. Anything that directly affects people needs someone accountable who has read it.

## Don't swing to the other extreme

It's tempting to conclude that AI output just isn't good enough.

That's too quick. We produce code and documents with AI every day, and most of it is good. The difference is whether someone checked it, and whether the bar slipped because "AI wrote it."

And the reverse: more output doesn't mean the team got more efficient. If the sender got faster and the receiver got slower, the whole team didn't necessarily speed up.

## Four rules for managers

1. Using AI is fine. The quality bar is the same as without it.
2. The person who hands it in owns the quality. "AI wrote it" is not an excuse.
3. Let risk decide how much human review: acceptance checks for low risk, people for high risk.
4. Anything meant for people to read must be read by a person first.
