> **TL;DR**: When enterprise AI adoption stalls at the POC, the model is rarely the problem. The sponsoring manager may not know the front-line work, the POC only touched clean data, and the people doing the job don't feel the benefit. Put those together and the POC ends at the demo. To move forward, pick a small, clear use case where someone can judge the output. Once the front line feels the win, the next use case grows from inside.

## How a POC usually starts

Most AI POCs I see start the same way. One motivated manager wants to bring AI in, and gets a team or a vendor to build a proof of concept.

The POC works, more or less. The demo looks good. Then it's time to roll it out to the people who'd actually use it, and everything slows down.

This isn't unique to any one market. In Deloitte's [State of AI in the Enterprise](https://news.designrush.com/why-enterprise-ai-fails-to-scale) survey this year, 84% of companies increased AI spending, but only 25% have moved 40% or more of their AI experiments into production. The money is spent and the experiments are run. The next step is where things stop.

## Three places it gets stuck

### The sponsor doesn't know what the front line actually does

The manager who wants AI usually isn't the person doing the work. They see the steps on the process diagram. The front line deals with what the diagram leaves out: customers who can't explain what they need, data that doesn't match across systems, rules you only learn by asking three people.

A POC built on the manager's version runs smoothly. Hand it to the front line and the first reaction is often, "That's not how it actually works."

### The POC only touched the surface, and the data isn't ready

To move fast, POCs usually use one clean dataset and a tidy set of examples. That's reasonable. POCs should be fast.

But going live, you find the data spread across several places, in different formats, some of it still in someone's head. I wrote about this in "[Don't Make AI Adoption the Goal Itself](/en/posts/dont-make-ai-adoption-the-goal/)": if the process and data aren't digitized, a stronger model won't help. A working POC only proves the clean-data part works.

### The people doing the work don't feel the benefit

This is the one most people underestimate.

If the people doing the day-to-day work don't feel AI helping them, right away and directly, they have no reason to change how they work. Learning a new tool, adjusting habits, cleaning up after AI mistakes: those are all costs to them. The benefit is a number on their manager's slide.

Without an immediate benefit, they won't keep using it. However good the POC was, it stays a demo.

## Cut a small, clear use case

So there are two ways to really bring AI into a company: much deeper integration, or a use case that's small and clear enough.

For most companies, the second is the realistic one. Starting small is how the POC actually gets done properly, and how people inside feel real results.

### How we pick the first use case

When a manager asks us what to start with, we ask them to list a few use cases they have in mind, then go through them one by one in the meeting.

For each, we align on two things: how far do you want this to go, and what data do you need to get there?

Then there's one condition that ends the conversation if it fails: **is there someone who can judge whether the POC results are good?**

If the manager can't find someone who really knows the work, or someone who can tell whether the AI's output is right, we recommend not starting there. If no stakeholder can judge the result, building it is just for fun. Without someone who can say "this answer is right, that one isn't," the POC never reaches a conclusion, and there's no next step.

## The next use case came from inside

One of our customers in education started with exactly one thing: grading student homework.

The scope was small, and the results were easy to judge. A teacher can tell at a glance whether the grading is right. After a while, the teachers felt the difference.

Then something interesting happened. The next use case wasn't our idea, and it wasn't the manager's plan. People inside came up with it. After seeing the grading results, they understood that AI is good at work that's well defined and repetitive. And they realized lesson preparation is a similar kind of work.

So the second use case became helping teachers prepare lessons.

That's what I mean when I say scale comes from running something, not from planning it. The real output of the first use case wasn't just the hours saved on grading. The front line formed its own sense of what AI is good at. With that, they find the next use case themselves, and it's usually a better pick than anything planned in a meeting room, because they know what they do every day.

## Showing results: it's still about money

Once it grows, the manager will be asked where the results are.

Honestly, the best way I've seen is still money. Take something the company already spends money or time on. After AI takes it on, how much cheaper is it, or how much faster?

That's also why "give everyone a license" is so hard to justify. With a seat per person, all you can see is usage. You can't see which cost it cut. Pick a specific workflow and you can do the math.

## Don't swing to the other extreme

It's tempting to conclude that POCs are useless and you should go straight to a big platform.

That's too quick. POCs are fine. A good one works like an executable spec reviewer, surfacing vague problems early. The problem is picking the wrong use case: too big, too vague, nobody to judge it, nothing the front line feels.

And the reverse: a beautiful demo doesn't mean adoption worked. The demo proves it can be built. Adoption is when the front line wants to use it every day.

## Four questions before you pick the first use case

1. Who does this work every day? Will they feel the benefit directly?
2. Is there someone who can judge whether the AI's output is right?
3. What data does it need to get where you want? Where is that data today?
4. What does this cost in money or time today? What number will you show leadership afterward?

If you can answer all four, it's worth being your first use case.
